# Cream City Almanac

**Look up your Milwaukee neighborhood. Every number says where it came from.**

www.creamcityalmanac.app

Milwaukee publishes a lot of information about itself. Crime reports, fire calls, property records, voter turnout, health surveys, census figures. Most of it sits in spreadsheets and map files that only a data analyst would open. Cream City Almanac puts it in one place and answers in plain English, so you can find out what's going on where you live without learning a spreadsheet.

It draws on three sources: Data You Can Use, which publishes Milwaukee neighborhood data and reports; the City of Milwaukee's open data; and Health Compass Milwaukee, from the Milwaukee Health Care Partnership.

## What you can do

**Type your address.** You'll see which neighborhood you're in, your alder and aldermanic district, your voting ward, and your polling place, with notes on its accessible entrance and parking. The address goes to the U.S. Census Bureau to find its location, and the Almanac doesn't keep it.

**Read your neighborhood's fact sheet.** For the 27 neighborhoods Data You Can Use profiles, a printable page shows poverty, income, renters, vacant homes, rent burden, car ownership and schooling, each one next to the citywide figure. A plain label says whether the neighborhood is clearly higher, clearly lower, or about the same as the city.

**Ask a question in your own words.** "How many robberies were there in Harambee this year?" "Which neighborhoods have the highest rent burden?" "How long have Milwaukee's vacant buildings been vacant?" Ask answers with a card that shows the number, its source, and its fine print. Some of what it can answer:

- Crime counts back to 2005, mapped and counted inside the City's own neighborhood boundaries
- Fire Department calls and response times since 2015
- How long City cases take, like 311 requests and permits, and how long vacant buildings have sat on the City's list
- Who lives in a ZIP code, and health, food and hospital-visit figures by ZIP code
- Which neighborhoods have the most of something, and whether two things go together, like renting and lack of home internet
- Voter turnout, police stops from 2019 to 2024, and how property values have changed since 2005

**Search the whole collection.** Type what you're curious about, like "kids who can't afford food," and the right dataset comes up. Every dataset has a plain-English page that explains what it measures, which years and places it covers, what to be careful about, and questions worth asking.

## Why you can trust the numbers

The AI in Cream City Almanac reads your question and picks the right tool. Code does the math. The AI never writes a number.

Every fact carries a small tag that says who wrote it: DYCU for Data You Can Use, CITY, CENSUS, HCM for Health Compass Milwaukee, or AI. Anything the AI wrote, like a plain-English summary, is labeled AI so you know to check it.

Survey figures come with their margin of error, so you can see how sure the estimate is. Caveats sit right next to the number they apply to.

Ask won't tell you anything about a specific property, owner or person. In City files that name people, it hides any group smaller than 10.

## For journalists

You're on deadline and need a number you can stand behind. Ask gives you the figure with its source, its time period and its caveats on one card. Export it as a spreadsheet, plain text or an image, with the source notes already attached. Or send a share link that anyone can open without signing in.

Every dataset page lists story angles to start from, and the caveats tell you what the data can and can't support before you write the sentence. The search also reads inside about 180 Data You Can Use reports, so you can find the passage that backs up a figure.

## For nonprofits

Grant applications want local numbers with a credible source. A neighborhood fact sheet gives you the headline figures next to the city's, with margins of error, and prints on one page. For something narrower, like Harambee's poverty numbers by age, open the neighborhood spreadsheet, pick the year and the table, and quote each estimate with its range and the Census table it came from.

Ask can compare neighborhoods for you ("Which neighborhoods have the highest rent burden?"), and the address lookup tells you which neighborhood, district and ward a site sits in.

## Good to know

- Search and fact sheets are open to everyone. Ask is free with a sign-in, up to 30 questions a day.
- The catalog refreshes every Monday, and 23 City datasets update daily.
- Survey numbers are estimates, so read the range along with the number.
- Cream City Almanac is unofficial and isn't affiliated with Data You Can Use or the City of Milwaukee. Questions about the data itself go to Data You Can Use at hub@datayoucanuse.org.
